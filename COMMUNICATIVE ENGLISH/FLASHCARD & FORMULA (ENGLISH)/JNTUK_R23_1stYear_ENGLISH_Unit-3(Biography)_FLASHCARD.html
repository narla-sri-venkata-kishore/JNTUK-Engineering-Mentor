    Q [10M]tap to reveal ▾
    Discuss the importance of studying biography as a literary genre for students.
    A ►
    • Definition: A biography is a detailed, factual account of a real person's life written by another person.
    • Educational value: Biographies expose students to real-world examples of success and failure, making abstract values like perseverance and integrity concrete.
    • Inspiration: Reading about figures like Musk shows students that extraordinary achievement begins with extraordinary dedication.
    • Language skills: Develops vocabulary, reading comprehension, inference skills, and ability to identify themes and character traits.
    • Critical thinking: Students learn to evaluate decisions, analyse cause-effect relationships, and form judgements about leadership and ethics.
    • Writing skills: Studying biography structure (chronological, thematic) helps students structure their own essays effectively.
    • Conclusion: Biographies bridge literature and life, making them one of the most educationally rich genres for academic and personal growth.

    A ►
    • Transformational leadership: Inspires employees with a grand vision that transcends daily tasks, creating deep organisational commitment.

    🎧 Listening Skills

    Global Comprehension · Summarising · Note-Taking

  • Listen for the MAIN IDEA first (global comprehension), not every word.
  • Identify KEY WORDS: nouns, verbs, dates, names, numbers — these carry core meaning.
  • Notice SIGNAL WORDS: 'however', 'therefore', 'in conclusion', 'for example' — they indicate idea shifts.
  • Use abbreviations while note-taking: w/ = with, eg = example, imp = important, → = leads to.
  • After listening, immediately summarise in 2–3 sentences without re-listening — this is active recall.
    • Q [Listening]tap to reveal ▾
    What is the difference between hearing and listening?
    A ►
    • Hearing is a passive, involuntary physical process of receiving sound waves through the ears.
    • Listening is an active, conscious process involving attention, comprehension, interpretation, and response.
    • Key difference: Hearing requires no effort; listening requires focused mental engagement.
    Q [Listening]tap to reveal ▾
    What are the four levels of listening comprehension?
    A ►
    • 1. Global comprehension: Understanding the overall topic and main idea.
    • 2. Selective comprehension: Identifying specific details, facts, or figures.
    • 3. Inferential comprehension: Understanding implied meanings not directly stated.
    • 4. Evaluative comprehension: Critically judging the speaker's purpose, bias, or credibility.
    Q [Listening]tap to reveal ▾
    What strategies help in effective note-taking while listening?
    A ►
    • Use the CORNELL METHOD: Divide paper into Notes (right), Cues (left), Summary (bottom).
    • Write only KEY PHRASES, not full sentences. Use arrows and symbols.
    • Leave BLANK SPACES to fill in missed points immediately after listening.
    • Number your points to maintain the sequence of ideas.
    • Review and rewrite notes within 24 hours while memory is fresh.
    Q [Listening]tap to reveal ▾
    What is global comprehension and why is it important in listening?
    A ►
    • Global comprehension refers to understanding the overall meaning, topic, and purpose of a spoken text without focusing on every individual word.
    • It provides the framework into which specific details fit and prevents losing the 'big picture'.
    • Technique: Before listening, read any questions available to set a listening purpose; during listening, focus on topic sentences and signal words.
    Q [Listening]tap to reveal ▾
    What signal words indicate a contrast or opposing idea in a spoken text?
    A ►
    • However / Nevertheless / On the other hand / Yet / Despite this / In contrast / Although / Even though / But / Whereas.
    • Example: 'Musk's early companies succeeded quickly. However, SpaceX faced three consecutive launch failures before achieving orbit.'
    • Recognising these signals helps listeners adjust their understanding and anticipate a shift in argument.

    🗣 Speaking Skills

    Group Discussions · Pair Discussions · Reporting

  • Initiation: Open with a definition or question. ('Let's begin by defining innovation...')
  • Development: Present your view with a reason and example. ('I believe... because... For instance...')
  • Agreement/Disagreement: Respond to others politely before adding your point.
  • Summarising: The reporter summarises the key points discussed at the close.
  • Key rule: Speak clearly, listen actively, do not dominate or interrupt aggressively.
    • Q [Speaking]tap to reveal ▾
    What is a group discussion and what are its key objectives?
    A ►
    • A group discussion (GD) is a structured oral activity where a small group (4–8 participants) examines a topic from multiple perspectives.
    • Objectives: Develop communication and listening skills; evaluate ability to think, articulate, and collaborate; practise agreeing and disagreeing politely; build confidence in formal oral communication.
    Q [Speaking]tap to reveal ▾
    Give 6 useful phrases for agreeing and disagreeing politely in a group discussion.
    A ►
    • Agreeing: 'I completely agree with your point because...' | 'That's an excellent observation. Additionally...' | 'You've raised a valid point. To add to that...'
    • Disagreeing: 'I see your perspective, however I think...' | 'With due respect, I'd like to offer an alternate view...' | 'While that is a valid point, we must also consider...'
    • Golden rule: Always acknowledge the other speaker's view before presenting your counter-argument.
    Q [Speaking]tap to reveal ▾
    How do you report the outcomes of a group discussion?
    A ►
    • Use third-person reporting language and formal tone.
    • Structure: (1) State the topic discussed; (2) Summarise the main points raised; (3) Note areas of agreement and disagreement; (4) State the group's conclusion.
    • Sample: 'Our group discussed electric vehicles. Most members agreed they are the future, citing environmental benefits. However, there was disagreement on affordability. The group concluded that government subsidies are essential.'
    Q [Speaking]tap to reveal ▾
    What should you do if you do not know the answer during a group discussion?
    A ►
    • DO NOT stay silent or make up facts.
    • Admit honestly: 'I don't have specific data on that, but based on general knowledge...'
    • Redirect: 'That's an interesting angle. Could we hear others' views first?'
    • Ask a question: 'What does the group think about...?' — shows engagement.
    • Silence is more damaging than an honest admission. Active listening and thoughtful questions are valued equally.
    Q [Speaking]tap to reveal ▾
    What is a pair discussion and how does it differ from a group discussion?
    A ►
    • A pair discussion involves two participants exchanging views on a given topic, typically for 5–10 minutes.
    • Pair = 2 people, more intimate, each person speaks longer; GD = 4–8 people, requires skills to manage turn-taking.
    • Common structure: Speaker A presents → Speaker B responds → Both explore a conclusion together.

    📚 Reading Skills

    Inference · Context Clues · Detailed Comprehension

  • Definition clue: Meaning directly stated. 'Philanthropy, the practice of donating to charitable causes...'
  • Synonym clue: Similar-meaning word nearby. 'His tenacity, or stubbornness, was legendary.'
  • Antonym/contrast clue: Opposite word signals meaning. 'Unlike cautious investors, Musk was audacious.'
  • Example clue: Examples illustrate the word. 'Musk invested in renewable ventures such as solar energy and electric vehicles.'
  • Inference clue: Meaning implied through surrounding sentences. Read 2 sentences before and after the unknown word.
    • Q [Reading]tap to reveal ▾
    What is an inference in reading comprehension?
    A ►
    • An inference is a logical conclusion drawn from evidence in the text that is not directly stated by the author.
    • It requires combining textual evidence with prior knowledge to arrive at an implied meaning.
    • Example: If a text says 'Musk slept on the factory floor during Tesla's production crisis,' we can INFER he was extremely dedicated — even though the text does not use the word 'dedication.'
    • Strategy: Ask 'What does this suggest?' rather than 'What does this say?'
    Q [Reading]tap to reveal ▾
    What is the difference between skimming and scanning?
    A ►
    • Skimming: Reading quickly to get the general idea or gist. Read the title, headings, first and last sentences. Used for: deciding if an article is relevant.
    • Scanning: Moving eyes rapidly to locate a specific piece of information (a date, name, statistic). Used for: answering a specific question without reading the whole passage.
    • Memory trick: Skim = Surface; Scan = Specific Search.
    Q [Reading]tap to reveal ▾
    Identify the context clue type and meaning: 'Musk's ventures were unprecedented; no entrepreneur had ever attempted anything like them before.'
    A ►
    • Type: Definition / Restatement Clue.
    • The phrase 'no entrepreneur had ever attempted anything like them before' directly explains 'unprecedented.'
    • Meaning: Never having happened or existed before; completely new and without comparison.
    • Technique: Look for punctuation (semicolons, commas, dashes) or phrases like 'that is', 'in other words', 'or' which signal a restatement.
    Q [Reading]tap to reveal ▾
    What are the steps to answer an inference-based comprehension question?
    A ►
    • Step 1: Read the question carefully — identify what is being asked (cause, consequence, attitude, implied meaning?).
    • Step 2: Locate the relevant section. Read 2–3 sentences around it.
    • Step 3: Identify key words and phrases that hint at the answer.
    • Step 4: Combine textual evidence with logical reasoning. Do NOT copy sentences directly.
    • Step 5: Write: 'The text implies... supported by the phrase... which indicates...'

    Q [Writing]tap to reveal ▾
    What are the steps to write an effective summary?
    A ►
    • Step 1: Read the passage carefully once for overall meaning.
    • Step 2: Identify the main idea and 3–4 key supporting points.
    • Step 3: Underline or note these key points.
    • Step 4: Put away the original. Write the summary entirely in your own words.
    • Step 5: Limit length to 1/3 of the original. Begin with 'The passage discusses...'
    • Step 6: Review — ensure no opinions, examples, or direct quotes from the original are included.
    Q [Writing]tap to reveal ▾
    How do you make effective notes from a reading passage?
    A ►
    • Write the heading/topic at the top. Divide into sub-headings (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How).
    • Use bullet points for key facts. Avoid full sentences.
    • Use abbreviations: eg., imp., re., &, →, approx., b/w (between).
    • Use symbols: ✓ for confirmed facts, ? for uncertain points, ★ for key points.
    • End with a brief summary line connecting all points.
    • Example: 'Musk, E. → Born: 1971, SA → Companies: SpaceX (2002), Tesla (2004) → Key trait: Resilience'
    Q [Writing]tap to reveal ▾
    What is paraphrasing and what are the techniques for effective paraphrasing?
    A ►
    • Paraphrasing means expressing someone else's ideas in your own words while retaining the original meaning.
    • Synonym substitution: Replace key words. 'innovative' → 'groundbreaking'; 'founded' → 'established'.
    • Sentence restructuring: Change structure. Active → Passive; Simple → Complex.
    • Change word forms: 'His innovation was significant' → 'He innovated significantly.'
    • Check: Cover the original. Does your paraphrase convey the same meaning? Are no more than 3 consecutive words identical?
    Q [Writing]tap to reveal ▾
    What are the most common mistakes students make in summary writing?
    A ►
    • Copying sentences directly from the original → Always rewrite in own words.
    • Including personal opinions ('I think...', 'In my view...') → Summaries are objective.
    • Adding examples not in the original → Summaries only contain what the source contains.
    • Writing more than 1/3 of the original length → Count words and trim.
    • Starting with 'This passage is about...' (weak opener) → Begin with the main idea directly.
    • Changing the meaning while paraphrasing → Verify meaning match after writing.

    📏 Grammar

    Tenses · Subject-Verb Agreement · Compound Words · Collocations

    Tenses
    Q [Grammar – Tenses]tap to reveal ▾
    When do we use the Simple Past Tense? Give 2 examples from Elon Musk's biography.
    A ►
    • Use Simple Past for completed actions at a specific time in the past. Structure: Subject + V2.
    • Example 1: 'Elon Musk founded SpaceX in 2002.'
    • Example 2: 'He sold PayPal to eBay for $1.5 billion in 2002.'
    • Signal words: yesterday, in [year], ago, last year, once, when.
    Q [Grammar – Tenses]tap to reveal ▾
    What is the difference between Simple Past and Past Perfect Tense?
    A ►
    • Simple Past: Single completed action. → 'Musk launched the Falcon 1 rocket.'
    • Past Perfect: Action completed before another past action. → 'By the time Tesla became profitable, Musk had invested his entire fortune in the company.'
    • Key signal: Past Perfect requires TWO past events; the earlier one uses 'had + V3'.
    • Memory trick: Past Perfect = 'the more past of two past events.'
    Q [Grammar – Tenses]tap to reveal ▾
    Correct the tense error: 'Before Musk starts SpaceX, he was already founded PayPal.'
    A ►
    • Correction: 'Before Musk started SpaceX, he had already founded PayPal.'
    • Error 1: 'starts' (present) → 'started' (Simple Past, the more recent of two past events).
    • Error 2: 'was already founded' (passive, incorrect) → 'had already founded' (Past Perfect, the earlier of two past events).
    Q [Grammar – Tenses]tap to reveal ▾
    When is the Present Perfect Tense used? Provide 2 exam-ready examples.
    A ►
    • Use for: (1) actions completed at an unspecified time in the past; (2) actions that began in the past and continue to the present; (3) life experiences.
    • Structure: Subject + has/have + V3
    • Example 1: 'SpaceX has successfully launched over 200 missions to date.'
    • Example 2: 'Elon Musk has transformed the electric vehicle industry.'
    • Signal words: already, yet, just, ever, never, since, for, so far, recently, to date.
    Subject-Verb Agreement
    Q [Grammar – SVA]tap to reveal ▾
    State the basic rule of Subject-Verb Agreement with examples.
    A ►
    • Rule: A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb.
    • Singular: 'Elon Musk IS a visionary entrepreneur.'
    • Plural: 'His companies ARE changing the world.'
    • TRICK: 'Each', 'every', 'either', 'neither', 'anyone', 'everyone', 'no one' are ALWAYS singular.
    • Example: 'Every one of his companies HAS a specific mission.' (NOT 'have')
    Q [Grammar – SVA]tap to reveal ▾
    Identify and correct the error: 'The team of engineers are working on the new rocket design.'
    A ►
    • Error: 'are' is incorrect.
    • Correction: 'The team of engineers IS working on the new rocket design.'
    • Rule: Collective nouns (team, committee, group, staff, board, jury) acting as a single unit take a SINGULAR verb.
    • Exception: If members act individually → plural verb. 'The team were divided in their opinions.'
    Q [Grammar – SVA]tap to reveal ▾
    Fill in the blank: 'Neither Musk nor his critics _____ (was/were) prepared for Tesla's success.'
    A ►
    • Answer: were
    • Rule: With 'Neither...nor' / 'Either...or', the verb agrees with the SUBJECT CLOSER TO THE VERB (the nearer subject rule).
    • 'his critics' is plural and is the nearer subject → plural verb 'were'.
    • Memory tip: 'The verb follows its nearest neighbour.'
    Compound Words & Collocations
    Q [Grammar – Compound]tap to reveal ▾
    What is a compound word? Give 5 examples from the context of technology and innovation.
    A ►
    • A compound word is formed by combining two or more words to create a new word with a distinct meaning.
    • Types: Closed (breakthrough, spacecraft, benchmark, feedback, output); Hyphenated (well-known, self-taught, state-of-the-art); Open (electric vehicle, solar panel, space station).
    • Technology examples: breakthrough, spacecraft, software, benchmark, workflow.
    Q [Grammar – Collocation]tap to reveal ▾
    What is a collocation? How is it different from a compound word?
    A ►
    • A collocation is a pair of words that frequently occur together and sound natural to native speakers.
    • Examples: 'make a decision' (NOT 'do a decision'); 'strong coffee' (NOT 'powerful coffee'); 'take a risk' (NOT 'make a risk').
    • Difference: Compound words create a new meaning ('breakthrough'). Collocations are natural word partnerships that must be memorised as fixed combinations.
    • Exam tip: 'Musk _____ a bold decision.' → MADE (not 'took' or 'did').

    📘 Glossary

    Key Terms from Biography, Skills & Grammar

    Autobiography
    A book or account written by a person about their own life. Differs from biography (written by another person).
    Biography
    A detailed written account of a person's life, written by another author using research, interviews, and records.
    Chronological order
    Arranging events in the sequence in which they occurred in time; the standard structure for biographies.
    Collocation
    A pair or group of words that habitually appear together in natural language (e.g., 'make a decision', 'heavy rain').
    Compound word
    A word formed by combining two or more existing words to create a new meaning (e.g., 'breakthrough', 'spacecraft').
    Context clue
    Words or phrases surrounding an unknown word that provide hints about its meaning.
    Global comprehension
    Understanding the overall meaning and main idea of a text or speech, rather than focusing on specific details.
    Inference
    A conclusion reached by reasoning from evidence in the text; an implied meaning not directly stated by the author.
    Innovation
    The process of introducing new ideas, devices, or methods; the act of creating something new or improved.
    Note-making
    Recording key information from a text or speech using abbreviated, organised, point-form notes.
    Paraphrase
    Restating a text in different words while preserving the original meaning, roughly the same length.
    Perseverance
    Continued effort and determination in doing something despite difficulty, failure, or opposition.
    Resilience
    The ability to recover quickly from setbacks, adapt positively to adversity, and continue forward.
    Signal word
    A word or phrase that indicates how ideas are connected (e.g., 'however' = contrast; 'therefore' = result).
    Simple Past Tense
    A verb tense used to describe actions completed at a specific time in the past. Structure: Subject + V2.
    Skimming
    Reading quickly through a text to get its general idea, focusing on headings and topic sentences.
    Scanning
    Reading to locate a specific piece of information quickly without reading the entire text.
    Subject-Verb Agreement
    The grammatical rule that a verb must agree in number (singular/plural) and person with its subject.
    Summary
    A brief statement of the main points of a text, written in one's own words, approximately 1/3 of the original length.
    Tense
    A form of a verb that indicates the time at which an action or state occurs (past, present, or future).
    Entrepreneurship
    The activity of setting up and running a business, particularly under conditions of financial risk.
    Visionary
    A person with clear, distinctive, and positive ideas about how things could or should be in the future.
    First-principles thinking
    A problem-solving approach that breaks down complex systems to their most basic truths and rebuilds solutions from there, ignoring conventional assumptions.
    Active recall
    A study technique in which the learner actively retrieves information from memory rather than passively re-reading material.
    Spaced repetition
    A learning technique involving reviewing material at increasing time intervals to improve long-term retention.
