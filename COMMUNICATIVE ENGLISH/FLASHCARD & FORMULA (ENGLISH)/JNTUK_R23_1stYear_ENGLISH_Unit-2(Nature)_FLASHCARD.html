Literature — The Brook

Tap any card to reveal the answer.

● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 01 | About the Author Who was Alfred Lord Tennyson?
● BACK — ANSWER
Alfred Lord Tennyson (1809–1892) was a celebrated Victorian poet and Poet Laureate of Great Britain. Known for musical verse and deep emotion, he wrote 'The Brook' as part of 'The Brook: An Idyl' (1855). Notable works include 'In Memoriam,' 'Ulysses,' and 'The Charge of the Light Brigade.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 02 | Summary Give a short summary of 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
The Brook is a dramatic monologue where a brook speaks about its journey from the hills to a river. It describes landscapes, plants, animals, and human settlements it passes through. The key contrast: men come and go, but the brook flows on forever. The poem celebrates the eternal flow of nature versus the temporary nature of human life.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 03 | Central Theme What are the main themes of 'The Brook'?
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Continuity of Nature: The brook symbolises nature's eternal journey. 2. Transience of Human Life: Humans are mortal; nature is not. 3. Joy and Energy of Nature: The brook is lively, noisy, joyful. 4. Progress and Movement: Life is a journey — never stagnant. Key Line: "For men may come and men may go, But I go on for ever."
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 04 | Central Idea What is the central idea of 'The Brook'?
● BACK — ANSWER
The central idea is that Nature is immortal and eternal while human life is short-lived and temporary. The brook personifies nature — energetic, free, and everlasting. Tennyson uses the brook as a metaphor for life's continuous flow, conveying that despite all changes, the forces of nature persist forever.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 05 | Important Lines Explain: "For men may come and men may go, But I go on for ever."
● BACK — ANSWER
Meaning: The brook states that while humans are born and die (temporary), the brook (nature) flows eternally without end. Literary Device: Antithesis (contrast between 'men' and 'I'), Repetition (refrain used 4 times). Significance: Summarises the poem's central theme of nature's immortality vs human mortality.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 06 | Important Lines Explain: "I chatter over stony ways, In little sharps and trebles"
● BACK — ANSWER
Meaning: The brook describes the sound it makes flowing over rocks. 'Sharps and trebles' are musical terms — the bubbling noise is compared to high-pitched musical notes. Literary Device: Personification (brook 'chatters'), Musical Imagery, Onomatopoeia ('chatter'). Significance: Shows the joyful, musical quality of the brook as it navigates obstacles.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 07 | Important Lines Explain: "I steal by lawns and grassy plots, I slide by hazel covers"
● BACK — ANSWER
Meaning: The brook silently and gracefully moves past green lawns and hazel shrubs. Literary Device: Personification ('I steal,' 'I slide'), Imagery (visual picture of nature). Significance: Shows the brook's gentle, quiet side — contrasting with its earlier noisy, energetic flow over stones.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 08 | Literary Devices – Set A Identify and explain THREE major literary devices in 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Personification: The brook is given human qualities — it speaks, chatters, bickers, murmurs, lingers. 2. Alliteration: Repetition of consonant sounds. "By thirty hills I hurry down." ('h' repeated) 3. Imagery: Vivid pictures of nature. 'Braid of bubbly sand,' 'netted sunbeam' — visual, auditory and tactile images.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 09 | Literary Devices – Set B Identify THREE more literary devices in 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
4. Repetition/Refrain: "For men may come and men may go, But I go on for ever" — repeated 4 times for emphasis. 5. Onomatopoeia: Words that imitate sounds — 'chatter,' 'murmur,' 'babble,' 'bicker.' 6. Metaphor: The brook is a metaphor for life's journey — beginning, various stages, and eventual end.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 10 | Journey of the Brook Describe the journey from source to destination.
● BACK — ANSWER
Origin: Springs from a mountain haunted by coot and hern, near a lonely farmhouse. Flow through Hills: Hurries past 30 hills, 20 thorpes, Philip's farm and a mill. Movement: Chatters over stones, slides past lawns and hazel bushes. Destination: Merges into 'the brimming river.' Throughout: Passes farms, bridges, flowers (forget-me-nots, mallows, kingcups), fish, and human settlements.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 11 | 2-Mark Q What does the brook symbolise in the poem?
● BACK — ANSWER
The brook symbolises Nature — its eternal, unceasing energy and movement. It is a symbol of immortality, continuous progress, and the joy of living. It also symbolises the journey of life: moving through difficulties, peaceful phases, and ultimately merging with something greater.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 12 | 2-Mark Q Name four poetic devices used in 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Personification – The brook speaks and acts like a human ('I chatter,' 'I linger'). 2. Alliteration – 'By thirty hills I hurry down' ('h' repeated). 3. Onomatopoeia – 'Chatter,' 'murmur,' 'babble' — words that sound like flowing water. 4. Repetition/Refrain – 'For men may come and men may go, But I go on for ever' (4 times).
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 13 | 10-Mark Q Write a critical appreciation of 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
Introduction: 'The Brook' (1855) is a lyrical monologue where Tennyson's brook narrates its own journey. Theme: Nature's immortality vs. human mortality. The refrain 'men may come, men may go' emphasises this. Structure: 9 stanzas; alternating narrative and brook monologue. Literary Devices: Personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia, imagery, repetition/refrain. Mood: Joyful, energetic, and philosophical. Conclusion: The poem beautifully captures the eternal nature of a stream. Individuals perish, but nature and its forces continue forever.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 14 | 10-Mark Q Analyse the use of nature imagery in 'The Brook.'
● BACK — ANSWER
Visual: 'netted sunbeam,' 'braid of bubbly sand,' 'forget-me-nots,' 'golden gravel,' 'silver'd silences.' Auditory: 'chatter,' 'babble,' 'murmur' — sounds of the brook. Tactile: 'shallow' water, 'stony ways.' Kinesthetic: Movement through 'twenty thorpes, a little town, half a hundred bridges.' Images serve two purposes: (1) vividly paint the English countryside; (2) mirror the brook's moods — noisy when excited, quiet when calm.
● FRONT — QUESTION
FC 15 | 2-Mark Q What does "I go on for ever" convey?
● BACK — ANSWER
The phrase conveys the brook's (and Nature's) immortality and endless continuity. Unlike humans who are born and die, the brook's flow never stops. It reflects the poem's philosophical message: nature is eternal while human life is brief. Repetition as a refrain reinforces the idea of never-ending movement.
Listening Skills

● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 01 | Definition What is the 'Main Idea' in a listening passage?
● BACK — ANSWER
The Main Idea is the central thought or primary message of the entire passage. It answers: 'What is this passage MOSTLY about?' • It is general (not specific). • It covers the overall point. • Usually stated at the beginning or end. Example: 'This passage is mainly about the importance of clean water for health.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 02 | Definition What is a 'Supporting Idea' in a listening passage?
● BACK — ANSWER
A Supporting Idea provides details, examples, or evidence that explains the main idea. It answers: 'What details support the main point?' • It is specific. • It cannot stand alone as the passage's meaning. • Multiple supporting ideas back up one main idea. Example: 'Only 3% of Earth's water is freshwater.' (supports water conservation.)
● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 03 | Technique How do you identify the Main Idea while listening?
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Listen to the FIRST and LAST sentences carefully. 2. Look for REPEATED words or ideas. 3. Ask: 'What is the whole passage trying to tell me?' 4. Eliminate very specific details (dates, names, numbers). 5. Predict from the title or introduction of the passage.
● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 04 | Technique What strategies help in answering listening-based questions?
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Preview: Read questions before listening if allowed. 2. Note-taking: Jot down keywords, not full sentences. 3. Listen for signal words: 'The main point is…,' 'Most importantly…,' 'In conclusion…' 4. Don't panic if you miss a word — context fills gaps. 5. Eliminate wrong options first in MCQs. 6. After listening, review notes and match with questions.
● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 05 | Practice 'Trees absorb CO₂, provide oxygen, prevent soil erosion, and support wildlife.' What is the main idea?
● BACK — ANSWER
Main Idea: Trees are essential for the environment. Supporting Ideas: • They absorb carbon dioxide. • They release oxygen. • They prevent soil erosion. • They provide habitat for wildlife. Note: The main idea covers ALL the details generally; each supporting idea covers only ONE specific benefit.
● FRONT — QUESTION
LS 06 | Signal Words Name six signal words used to introduce the main idea or supporting details.
● BACK — ANSWER
Main Idea Signals: • 'The main point is…' | 'Most importantly…' • 'In summary…' | 'The purpose of…' Supporting Detail Signals: • 'For example…' | 'In addition…' • 'Furthermore…' | 'According to…' • 'Firstly / Secondly / Finally' • 'This is because…'
Speaking Skills

● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 01 | Group Discussion What is a Group Discussion (GD) and how should you participate?
● BACK — ANSWER
A Group Discussion is a structured conversation where participants exchange ideas and reach conclusions. Do's: Speak clearly; build on others' points; use facts and examples; take turns; conclude with a balanced statement. Don'ts: Don't interrupt rudely; don't dominate or stay silent; don't go off-topic.
● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 02 | Structured Talk What is the format for a short structured talk (2–3 minutes)?
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Introduction (20 sec): Greet, state your topic. 'Good morning. I'd like to speak about…' 2. Main Body (90–100 sec): 2–3 key points with brief examples. Use linkers: 'Firstly… Secondly… Moreover…' 3. Conclusion (20–30 sec): Summarise. 'In conclusion, I believe that…' Tips: Maintain eye contact. Avoid filler words ('um,' 'uh'). Speak with confidence and pauses.
● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 03 | Sample Topic How would you open a GD on 'Is technology a boon or a bane?'
● BACK — ANSWER
Sample Opening: 'Good morning, everyone. Technology is undeniably one of the greatest achievements of human civilisation. It has transformed healthcare, education, and communication. However, it has also led to addiction, cybercrime, and job displacement. I believe technology is a boon when used wisely, and a bane when misused. I invite the group to explore both sides.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 04 | Expressing Opinion What phrases help you express your opinion clearly?
● BACK — ANSWER
To Express Opinion: • 'In my opinion…' | 'I strongly believe that…' • 'From my perspective…' | 'My view is that…' To Agree: • 'I completely agree with…' | 'That's an excellent point…' To Disagree Politely: • 'I see your point, but…' • 'With due respect, I think…' • 'I partially agree, however…'
● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 05 | GD Topics List 5 common GD topics for B.Tech English exam preparation.
● BACK — ANSWER
1. 'Social media is doing more harm than good.' 2. 'Online education vs. classroom education.' 3. 'Should plastic be completely banned?' 4. 'Environment vs. development — which is a priority?' 5. 'The role of youth in nation-building.' Prepare for each: (a) definition, (b) 2 points for, (c) 2 points against, (d) balanced conclusion.
● FRONT — QUESTION
SP 06 | Common Mistakes What are common speaking mistakes and how to correct them?
● BACK — ANSWER
Speaking too fast → Slow down; pause between ideas. Saying 'um/uh' → Replace with a brief pause or 'Let me think…' Looking at feet → Maintain eye contact. No structure → Always use Introduction–Body–Conclusion. Repeating the same point → Move forward with new ideas or examples.
Reading Skills

Tap any card to reveal the answer.

● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 01 | Sequence of Ideas What is 'sequence of ideas' in a paragraph?
● BACK — ANSWER
Sequence of Ideas is the logical order in which thoughts or arguments are arranged in a passage. Types: • Chronological: Events in time order (first… then… finally). • Logical: Cause → Effect; Problem → Solution. • Spatial: Describing locations (above, below, beside). • Order of Importance: Most important idea first or last.
● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 02 | Logical Flow What is logical flow in reading comprehension?
● BACK — ANSWER
Logical Flow means ideas move smoothly from one point to the next in a meaningful order. How to identify: 1. Each sentence must connect to the previous one. 2. Transitional words guide the reader ('however,' 'therefore,' 'in contrast'). 3. Topic sentence introduces; supporting sentences develop; concluding sentence closes.
● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 03 | Verbal Linking What are verbal linking techniques? Give examples.
● BACK — ANSWER
Verbal Linking (cohesive devices) connects ideas smoothly: 1. Addition: And, Also, Moreover, Furthermore, Besides 2. Contrast: But, However, Although, Nevertheless 3. Cause/Effect: Because, Therefore, Thus, Hence 4. Sequence: First, Then, Next, After that, Finally 5. Example: For instance, For example, Such as 6. Summary: In conclusion, To sum up, In brief
● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 04 | Practice Arrange in logical order: (A) She heated water. (B) She made tea. (C) She added tea leaves. (D) She felt thirsty.
● BACK — ANSWER
Correct Order: D → A → C → B Explanation: • D: First, she felt thirsty (cause). • A: Then she heated water (first action). • C: Next, she added tea leaves (second action). • B: Finally, she made tea (result). This is Chronological Sequence — events in the order they happened.
● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 05 | Skimming vs Scanning What is the difference between skimming and scanning?
● BACK — ANSWER
SKIMMING: • Purpose: Get a general overview/main idea quickly. • How: Read only headings, first lines, last lines. • When: 'What is this about?' SCANNING: • Purpose: Find a specific piece of information. • How: Move eyes quickly looking for keywords. • When: Searching for a date, name, number, or specific fact. Tip: Skim first, then scan to answer specific questions.
● FRONT — QUESTION
RS 06 | Topic Sentence What is a topic sentence? How do you identify it?
● BACK — ANSWER
A Topic Sentence states the main idea of a paragraph (usually the first sentence). How to identify: • It is general (not a detail). • The rest of the paragraph supports it. • It answers: 'What is this paragraph about?' Example: 'Pollution is one of the most serious threats to human health.' ← Topic Sentence 'It causes respiratory diseases, water contamination…' ← Supporting details
Writing Skills

● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 01 | Paragraph Structure What are the three parts of a well-written paragraph?
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Topic Sentence (Opening): Introduces the main idea. 'Regular exercise improves both physical and mental health.' 2. Supporting Details (Body): Facts, examples, reasons, explanations. 'It strengthens muscles, boosts immunity, and releases endorphins.' 3. Concluding Sentence (Closing): Summarises or restates the main idea. 'Therefore, making exercise a daily habit leads to a healthier life.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 02 | Sample Paragraph Write a paragraph on 'The Importance of Reading.'
● BACK — ANSWER
Reading is one of the most valuable habits a person can develop. It expands vocabulary, sharpens the mind, and exposes readers to new ideas, cultures, and perspectives. Studies show that regular readers have stronger critical thinking skills and better memory. Moreover, reading reduces stress and improves focus — both essential for academic success. In conclusion, cultivating a reading habit from an early age is an investment that pays lifelong dividends.
● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 03 | Common Mistakes List 5 common paragraph-writing mistakes and their corrections.
● BACK — ANSWER
1. No topic sentence → Always start with a clear main idea. 2. Only one sentence → Add at least 3–4 supporting details. 3. Going off-topic → Every sentence must relate to the topic sentence. 4. No conclusion → End with a summarising thought. 5. Run-on sentences → Use full stops and varied sentence lengths. Bonus: Starting every sentence with 'I' → Vary beginnings with 'Moreover,' 'However,' 'This shows that…'
● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 04 | Linkers How do linkers improve paragraph writing? Give examples.
● BACK — ANSWER
Linkers connect ideas logically and make writing coherent: Addition: 'Furthermore, exercise also helps with sleep quality.' Contrast: 'However, not all students have access to sports facilities.' Cause-Effect: 'As a result, regular readers perform better academically.' Example: 'For instance, Tennyson used personification to bring nature alive.' Conclusion: 'In conclusion, balanced nutrition is essential for good health.' Rule: Never start a paragraph with 'Also' or 'And.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 05 | Types of Paragraphs Name and briefly explain four types of paragraphs.
● BACK — ANSWER
1. Descriptive: Describes a person, place, or thing using sensory details. Topic: 'Describe a monsoon evening.' 2. Narrative: Tells a story or sequence of events. Topic: 'A day I will never forget.' 3. Expository: Explains or informs about a topic. Topic: 'Explain how photosynthesis works.' 4. Argumentative/Persuasive: Presents a point of view with evidence. Topic: 'Social media should be restricted for teenagers.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
WS 06 | 10-Mark Practice Write a paragraph on 'Nature as a Teacher.'
● BACK — ANSWER
Nature is humanity's oldest and most patient teacher. The seasons teach us about change — spring brings hope, summer brings energy, autumn reminds us to let go, winter teaches patience. The brook, as Tennyson describes, teaches resilience: it chatters over stones, slides past obstacles, but never stops. Trees model selflessness by providing shade, oxygen, and fruit without asking anything in return. Ultimately, the greatest lesson nature teaches is humility — that humans are not masters of the earth, but its caretakers.

● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 01 | Cohesive Devices What are cohesive devices? List them with examples.
● BACK — ANSWER
Cohesive devices link sentences and paragraphs logically: • Addition: Moreover, Furthermore, In addition, Besides • Contrast: However, Nevertheless, On the other hand, Although • Cause/Effect: Therefore, Thus, Hence, As a result • Time/Sequence: First, Then, Subsequently, Finally • Example: For example, For instance, Such as • Summary: In conclusion, To summarise, In brief Practice: 'She studied hard. _______, she failed.' → However / Nevertheless
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 02 | Articles Explain the rules for using A, An, The, and Zero Article.
● BACK — ANSWER
A (Indefinite): Before consonant sounds. 'a book,' 'a university' (u = 'yoo' sound). An (Indefinite): Before vowel sounds. 'an apple,' 'an hour' (h is silent). The (Definite): Specific/known nouns; superlatives; unique things. 'The sun,' 'the tallest building,' 'the book you mentioned.' Zero Article: • Plural general nouns: 'Dogs are loyal.' • Abstract nouns: 'Honesty is the best policy.' • Languages, sports, meals: 'I play cricket. I had breakfast.'
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 03 | Article Errors Correct these errors: (a) She is honest girl. (b) I saw a lion at zoo. (c) He is best student of an university.
● BACK — ANSWER
(a) She is an honest girl. ✓ ('honest' begins with vowel sound 'o.') (b) I saw a lion at the zoo. ✓ ('zoo' is a specific/known place → use 'the.') (c) He is the best student of a university. ✓ ('university' begins with 'yoo' (consonant sound) → 'a,' not 'an.')
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 04 | Prepositions Explain the difference between 'in,' 'on,' and 'at' with examples.
● BACK — ANSWER
IN (larger spaces / enclosed areas): • Time: in the morning, in 2024, in January • Place: in a room, in Mumbai, in India ON (surfaces / specific days): • Time: on Monday, on 15th August, on a rainy day • Place: on the table, on the wall, on the road AT (precise points / specific times): • Time: at 5 PM, at noon, at midnight • Place: at the bus stop, at home, at the corner Tip: AT (pin-point) → ON (surface) → IN (enclosed space)
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 05 | Preposition Errors Correct: (a) She arrived at Monday. (b) I live in Park Street. (c) Meeting is on 3 PM.
● BACK — ANSWER
(a) She arrived on Monday. ✓ (Days of the week use 'on.') (b) I live on Park Street. ✓ ('On' for streets; 'at' for specific addresses.) (c) The meeting is at 3 PM. ✓ ('At' for specific clock times.) Remember: IN the morning / ON Monday morning / AT 9 AM on Monday morning.
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 06 | Error Correction Correct: (a) He go to school everyday. (b) She have many books. (c) I am knowing the answer.
● BACK — ANSWER
(a) He goes to school every day. ✓ (he/she/it + 'goes'; 'every day' = adverb phrase, not 'everyday'.) (b) She has many books. ✓ (Third person singular: has, not have.) (c) I know the answer. ✓ (Stative verbs like 'know,' 'believe,' 'understand' are NOT used in continuous tense.)
● FRONT — QUESTION
GR 07 | Tense Errors Correct: (a) Yesterday I have eaten rice. (b) She is working here since 2020. (c) By tomorrow I will finished the report.
● BACK — ANSWER
(a) Yesterday, I ate rice. ✓ ('Yesterday' = definite past → Simple Past, not Present Perfect.) (b) She has been working here since 2020. ✓ ('Since' with ongoing action → Present Perfect Continuous.) (c) By tomorrow, I will have finished the report. ✓ ('By + future time' → Future Perfect tense.)
